Appendix B — Arrests

Below is full documentation of the arrest data as used by BLN and available on our github site. We have also created simpler subsets of the data to support specific stories, which are documented in those sections.

You should note that the date of arrest isn’t exact. They sometimes don’t log the arrest until someone is processed at an initial detention center, or when they are sent to immigration court.

About x percent of the rows in this dataset do not contain unique identifiers for the person. This happens when the person has never been part of the immigration enforcement system before (based usually on fingerprint matches). Beginning in April 2025, everyone who has been in the U.S. for at least 30 days must have an A-number. This requirement was always a possibility in the past, based on immigration laws, but wasn’t used for everyone until last year.

This happens most frequently with people who crossed the border illegally and have never been caught by immigration officials before.

Definitions

ERO administrative arrests

The Deportation Data project is acquired from ICE, and refers to “ERO administrative arrests”.

In his series “A close look at ICE arrest data..”, Austin Kocher says that the Deportation Data Project’s arrest data is actually administrative arrests reported by ICE’s Enforcement and Removal Operations. This excludes CBP, HSI, and non-administrative arrests, he says. It suggests it excludes people caught up in protests or arrested without immigration orders.

Generally, ICE’s ERO arrests people with civil immigration law violations - they’re based on administrative arrest warrents. A CRS report says they can make arrests in two instances when they don’t have a warrant: 1) The alien is attempting to enter the country, 2) the officer has “reason to believe” the immigrant is here unlawfully and will escape if not arrested.

This second exception suggests that ICE just has to say, “I think you’re here unlawfully” and they can arrest without a warrant. These are the brief stops now called “Kavanaugh stops” because he said that using a broad definition here, even racial profiling, can fall under that exception.

Apprehension method

Generally, people have tried to distinguish arrests that came from some kind of detention from those that happened in the wild. Different groups have done it differently:

Other stories

LAT, the City and others have distinguished between “street arrests” and others, but they’ve used different definitions.

ICE itself

I am using the categorization suggested by the 2024 ICE annual report, page 15:

  • At-large arrests, which take place within the community (i.e., outside of jails or prisons).
  • Custodial arrests, which take place in the confines of a jail or prison, as CAP works with federal, state and local law enforcement partners to take custody of removable noncitizens who have already been arrested by another law enforcement agency for criminal activity

Handling duplicates

There are several types of duplicates, and I’m handling them in different ways. Generally,

  1. If there is no id code for the person, I NEVER count it as a duplicate. There’s no way to tell if two people from the same country of the same age were arrested at the same time, or if it’s the same person twice.
  2. If the same person is arrested more than once within 24 hours, I count the earlier one as a duplicate. This could concievably happen, but it would be weird.
  3. If the same person is arrested twice at the same time, I count the one that comes first in the dataset as the duplicate. This could be wrong, and it can end up losing some data in a small number of rows.

There are other arrests, detentions and removals that are of the same person, but not duplicates – they appear to actually have been arrested or removed twice, for example. I’m keeping those, but noting which one is the last one logged.
